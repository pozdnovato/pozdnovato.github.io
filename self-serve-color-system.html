Self-Serve Color System for a Handmade Incense Line

HIPPIEMALL is a specialty wellness and lifestyle brand offering natural incense, Palo Santo, herbal smudge bundles, and oil perfumes, sold across major e-commerce platforms. The company was launching a new fragrance line, scaling toward hundreds of SKUs. My task was to design the label and to give the client a tool for producing new artwork on their own.

Task

A handmade incense brand was launching a new scent line. The task was to design a label for it: one identity that could carry the whole range as it expanded.

Process

I started with sketches, walked the client through directions, and we aligned on one. From there I designed the 38 labels that existed at that point. Each sharing a fixed layout but changing two things per scent: the background color and the text color.

The labels looked good because the background and text colors were paired by a designer with a solid grasp of color theory (me). I could not simply hand such a template over to the client. I needed to come up with a tool that would make this choice without relying on the designer.

Each label uses exactly two colors. The background color comes first and is chosen freely, by eye, based on the character of the scent (lemon is yellow, sandalwood is brown, lavender is green or purple). The text color is then chosen to contrast with the background.

The system had to support a really large number of labels and a wide variety of color pairs, since the client was talking about hundreds of scents. I decided that a single pairing rule (for example, “text is always a darker monochrome”) would not be enough.

I settled on four pairing strategies: monochrome (same hue, shifted in tone), analog (a neighboring hue), complement (the opposite), and accent (an unexpected pop).

But simply rotating the hue wheel was not enough on its own. A hue shift alone does not guarantee that the text stays readable against the background. Readability requires a difference in lightness. The catch is that in most color models, pushing lightness far enough for contrast also washes the color out. The text ends up reading as nearly black or nearly white, and the hue gets lost.

At that point I knew the tool had to be an InDesign script, and I started working on it with Claude. The key was to describe the logic in OKLCH, a color model where lightness and chroma are independent. The text color keeps moderate lightness and high chroma. Contrast comes from the difference in lightness, not from diluting the color. Saturation stays vivid and the hue stays visible.

The whole algorithm now sits under the hood of the script. The client picks a background color, selects a frame filled with that color, and the script offers four color pair options. The text color can be switched between dark and light for extra variety. If needed, the background CMYK values can be edited right in the script dialog. Confirming a pair writes two matching CMYK swatches straight into the document, ready to use.

Result

The client now produces new, on-brand labels themselves, in InDesign, from a 8-option picker. Because the rules are encoded rather than explained, the output stays consistent as the line grows, and the design judgment that used to live only in my head now lives as an algorithm the brand owns.

I left behind not only a label, but the system that lets the client keep designing them.
